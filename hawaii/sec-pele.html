    Activity 2.3 Pele (15 min)

    Checklist.

    • Proportionality problems.
      🔗
    • Encourage multiple ways to solve the problem.
      🔗
    • Can be used as a thermometer (challenge problem).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This activity includes two different problems about making sense of proportionality situations, and solving them flexibly. It will also demand students to think about their own bodies and scale them.
    🔗
    In the first problem, a large statue of Pele is to be made, and we know a range for its height, in feet. Students will need to first make sense of the table. They may first think that they just need to convert their own body’s measures to feet, without realizing that the table is relative to the giant statue.
    🔗
    To help students, you may tell them: “Imagine that YOU are the giant Pele, and you are 45 feet tall. If you created a measurement of 1 poho using your body, and then measured it using a large ruler, how many feet would you get?” [It may also help to ask: how many times as tall are you now?]
    🔗
    Students may also have trouble working with ranges. You may tell students to first simplify the problem and pretend that we are not given a range, but a single value (such as 45 or 50), and then make sense of the range. This strategy may actually be more efficient.
    🔗
    The second problem is similar, but now with a shrunken figure.
    🔗
    Students may think that there is a unique answer (as a typical closed mathematical problem), but the answer will actually depend on the relative body measurements that they assume Pele has! However, students should still compare their answers and they should be reasonably close. If they find large discrepancies, this is an opportunity to backtrack and see where their initial assumptions were so different (assuming that the calculations were correct).
    🔗

    Worksheet 2.3.1 Pele
    

    In Hawaiian religion, Pele is the goddess of volcanoes and fire and the creator of the Hawaiian Islands. Often referred to as “Madame Pele” or “Tūtū Pele” as a sign of respect, she is a well-known deity within Hawaiian mythology and is notable for her contemporary presence and cultural influence as an enduring figure from ancient Hawaii.
    🔗
    Legend told that Pele herself journeyed on her canoe from the island of Tahiti to Hawai’i. When on her journey, it was said she tried to create her fires on different islands, but her sister, Nāmaka, was chasing her, wanting to put an end to her. In the end, the two sisters fought each other and Pele was killed. With this happening, her body was destroyed but her spirit lives in Halema’uma’u on Kīlauea. They say, “Her body is the lava and steam that comes from the volcano. She can also change form, appearing as a white dog, old woman, or beautiful young woman.”
    🔗
    ―Adapted from Wikipedia
    🔗

    1. Sculpture of Pele.

    You want to make a sculpture of Pele. You want it to be between 45 and 50 feet tall. Complete the table of ranges with the Hawaiian measures relative to the statue.
    🔗
    You can think of it as follows: if a giant person is 45–50 ft tall, what is the length, in ft, of 1 poho of that person?

    2. A new Pele.

    You want to make a small figure of Pele. The length of 1 kīko’o of this figure is 1.25 inches. What should be the height of the figure, in inches?
    🔗
    Solution.

    1. Sculpture of Pele.

    Here are two common approaches students may take to complete Exercise 1:
    🔗
    🔗

    Approach 1: Doing a “me” table first.

    The student first finds all measures relative to themselves. For example, the student knows their height: 5.25 feet. Then, the student takes their Hawaiian measures using a tape measure, in inches, and converts to feet. The student may just take one measure, and use the conversion table from Part i to figure out the rest!
    🔗
    Finally, the student scales the measures. Since 45/5.25 is about 8.5, and 50/5.25 is about 9.5, the student scales multiplying by 8.5 and by 9.5 to complete Pele’s table.
    🔗
    myself giant Pele
    unit how long? (feet) how long? (feet)
    1 poho = \(\ldots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\ldots\) to \(\ldots\)
    1 kīko’o = \(\ldots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\ldots\) to \(\ldots\)
    1 ha’ilima = \(\ldots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\ldots\) to \(\ldots\)
    1 iwilei = \(\ldots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\ldots\) to \(\ldots\)
    height = 5.25 \(\Rightarrow\) 45 to 50
    🔗

    Approach 2: Finding height relative to a Hawaiian measure.

    The student uses their own body to estimate their height (or the one of a partner) in terms of Hawaiian measures.
    🔗
    Example: Carlos and Lucy are working together. Carlos helps Lucy figure out her height in terms of kīko’o. Say, they got 8.3. Then, they recall the nice relative relations between Hawaiian measurements (powers of 2). They first divide 45 (and 50) by 8.3 to get about 5.4 and 6.
    🔗

    height = 45 to 50
    Then, they divide by 2 to get smaller units (poho) and multiply by 2 and 4 to get larger units (ha’ilima and iwilei) to complete the table.
    🔗
    unit how long? (feet)
    1 poho = 2.7 to 3
    1 kīko’o = 5.4 to 6
    1 ha’ilima = 10.8 to 12
    1 iwilei = 21.6 to 24
    height = 45 to 50
    🔗

    2. A new Pele.

    Using the same proportional body ratio as before (height is approximately 8.3 kīko’o):
    🔗
    If 1 kīko’o is \(1.25\) inches, we can multiply by the person’s height in kīko’o:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Height} = 8.3 \times 1.25\text{ inches} \approx 10.375\text{ inches} \end{equation*}
    Alternatively, if a student uses their own ratio (for example, height is about 8 kīko’o):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Height} = 8 \times 1.25\text{ inches} = 10\text{ inches} \end{equation*}
