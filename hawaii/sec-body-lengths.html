    Activity 1.1 Body Lengths (20 min)

    Checklist.

    • Introduce the 4 lengths: ha’ilima, iwilei, kīko’o, poho
      🔗
    • Practice learning them using spinners, creating equations.
      🔗
    🔗
    Point students to the Student Workbook pages for Worksheet 1.1 Measuring With Our Bodies and Worksheet 1.1 Lengths! and have them make sense of the pictures that they see. Offer help only when needed, but otherwise have them lead in the explanations and observations about these non-standard lengths. Make sure that the following ideas emerge (either by students, but if not, you can lead):
    🔗
    • The words used are in Hawaiian language (www.gohawaii.com).
      🔗
    • We do NOT need a ruler for these measures. We need our body!
      🔗
    • These measures depend on the person. They are not the same for everyone, but should be close, say, for kids of the same age.
      🔗
    • If I use these measures, I should not expect exact results, because when using my body, maybe my hand is not fully stretched out, or I leave gaps measuring again.
      🔗
    • These measures seem practical for measuring things outside, maybe when building something, or doing tasks with hands.
      🔗
    • It seems complicated that there are 4 measures. I wonder if it would be best using just 1 or 2 measures…
      🔗
    • I wonder how these measures relate to inches, feet, meters, or other measures that we explored last time.
      🔗
    • I wonder which other body measures they use in that culture.
      🔗
    Have students work on Worksheet 1.1 Measuring With Our Bodies, and discuss the strategies used to get these values. Then, move to Worksheet 1.1 Lengths!. The first questions are closed ended, and just require one number to be filled out. Students may discuss whether they can place a decimal value (maybe 1.92, or 2.1, etc.), but have them also come up with an integer. The last two questions allow students to be more creative and come up with their own comparisons. In the last one, press students to use a number different than 1. Use body act outs and diagrams to make sense of the equations.
    🔗
    In Activity 1.2, students go deeper and build a measuring tape. You may return to Worksheet 1.1 Lengths! to revisit after this!
    🔗

    Worksheet Measuring With Our Bodies
    

    In Hawaiian culture, the body was commonly used to measure things like the length of a canoe, the height of a person, or the width of a taro patch. Using body lengths was a convenient way for the Hawaiians to estimate distances without instruments.
    🔗
    Imagine we are in the middle of the ocean without any rulers or measuring tapes. We want to know how long our canoe is in order to arrange our cargo.
    🔗

    1.

    Using the Hawaiian measures, what measurement could we use to most efficiently measure this canoe?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sample responses:
    🔗
    • “I would use iwilei because it is the largest measure that I see. Using small hand spans for a big canoe would take way too long.”
      🔗
    • “Iwilei works great, but even that takes many steps. I wonder if there’s an even bigger measure, like using full open arms, for long canoes?”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Estimate: About how many of that measurement would be equal to these 16 feet?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sample solutions:
    🔗
    • “I think about five iwilei, or perhaps a little more”
      🔗
    • “I think that an iwilei is about 3 feet long, so it would take around 5 or 6 iwilei to measure a 16-foot canoe.”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Discuss.

    1. What other things do you think the iwilei/ha’ilima/muku were used to measure?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. How might you use these measurements in your daily life?
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Sample responses:
    • “These body-based measures were maybe used for sizing woven mats, fishing nets, lengths of rope, or building things like canoes and shelters.”
      🔗
    • “They could have been used to measure fabrics for clothing, agricultural fields, or tracking the depth of shallow water.”
      🔗
    • “I could use my arm span or forearm to quickly check if a desk or rug will fit in my room without using a tape measure.”
      🔗
    • “I can use my iwilei or hand spans when shopping for craft supplies, wrapping ribbon, or measuring fabric at a store.”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    For reference, and when monitoring this activity, it’s helpful to know that, approximately, \(1\) anana = \(2\) iwilei = \(4\) ha’ilima = \(8\) kīko’o = \(16\) poho. See the figure below.

    Worksheet Lengths!
    

    1. Notebook length.

    Measure the long side of a notebook in poho, and then in kīko’o. What did you get approximately?
    • poho
      🔗
    • kīko’o
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sample responses (answers will vary depending on hand size):
    • 3 poho
      🔗
    • 1.5 (or 1 to 2) kīko’o
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Fill in the blanks:
    • 1 kīko’o is about the same as poho.
      🔗
    • 4 poho is about the same as iwilei.
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sample responses (other near values are acceptable):
    • 1 kīko’o is about the same as \(2\) poho.
      🔗
    • 4 poho is about the same as \(1/2\) iwilei (or \(0.4\) to \(0.6\)).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Create more comparisons:
    • 1 is about the same as
      🔗
    • is about the same as
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sample responses (other near values are acceptable):
    • 1 ha’ilima is about the same as 4 poho.
      🔗
    • 3 kīko’o is about the same as 6 poho.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Discuss.

    1. Why/when do you think these units of measure were invented?
      🔗
    2. What are the advantages of these units? And disadvantages?
      🔗
    3. Do you use your body or objects to take measures? How?
